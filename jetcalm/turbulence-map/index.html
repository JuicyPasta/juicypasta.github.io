What the colours mean

LevelWhat it feels like in the cabin
CalmNothing to notice, or an occasional gentle wobble.
LightSmall bumps, like a car on a gravel road. Drinks stay in the cup. Very common.
ModerateClear bumps and some jolts. Drinks can spill and walking is awkward. The seatbelt sign is usually on.
StrongFirm jolts that press you against the belt. Loose items move. Service stops. Uncommon.
SevereAbrupt, large movements. Rare, and pilots plan routes to stay away from areas where it is forecast.

The levels come from EDR (eddy dissipation rate), the standard measure of rough air in aviation forecasts, sorted into bands for a medium-sized airliner: light from an EDR of 0.15, moderate from 0.22, strong from 0.34 and severe from 0.45.

About this turbulence map

Four times a day, the world's aviation forecast centres publish the World Area Forecast System: a global forecast of turbulence at the heights airliners fly, in three-hour steps. This map shows the step nearest to now, at each place taking the strongest of the three cruise levels FL300, FL340 and FL390, on a half-degree grid (about 55 km). Because it shows the bumpiest of three heights, it looks rougher than any single flight will be: pilots pick the smoother height and route around the worst areas.

Turbulence is uncomfortable, but it is not dangerous to the aircraft: airliners are built for far more than they meet in flight. Keep your seatbelt fastened whenever you are seated. This map is for passengers; it is not a flight-planning tool, and Jetcalm is not affiliated with any airline.
