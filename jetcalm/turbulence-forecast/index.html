      What the levels mean

      LevelWhat it feels like in the cabin
      CalmNothing to notice, or an occasional gentle wobble.
      LightSmall bumps, like a car on a gravel road. Drinks stay in the cup. Very common.
      ModerateClear bumps and some jolts. Drinks can spill and walking is awkward. The seatbelt sign is usually on.
      StrongFirm jolts that press you against the belt. Loose items move. Service stops. Uncommon.
      SevereAbrupt, large movements. Rare, and pilots plan routes to stay away from areas where it is forecast.
      No dataThe forecast does not cover this stretch. A gap means no data, not calm.

      The levels come from EDR (eddy dissipation rate), the standard measure of rough air that aviation forecasts use, sorted into bands for a medium-sized airliner: light from an EDR of 0.15, moderate from 0.22, strong from 0.34 and severe from 0.45.

      How this turbulence forecast works

      Four times a day, the world's aviation forecast centres publish the World Area Forecast System (WAFS): a global forecast of turbulence and thunderstorm cloud at the heights airliners fly, in three-hour steps. This page draws the shortest path (the great circle) between your two airports, works out where the aircraft should be at each minute from your departure time, and reads the forecast at that place, time and height.

      • Climb and descent (the first 20 and last 25 minutes, or 15% of a short flight each) use the lower flight levels, FL140 to FL270.
      • Cruise uses a typical cruising height, FL340. "Could reach" is the strongest value nearby and at the other cruise heights (FL300 to FL390), because real flights don't follow the line exactly.
      • Thunderstorms: when large storm clouds are forecast near your path at cruise, the forecast says so. Pilots route around them, which can add a few minutes.
      • Flight time is estimated from the distance (about 830 km/h plus 30 minutes for taxi, climb and approach).

      How far ahead can I check?

      The free forecast covers flights that take off in the next 48 hours: that is how far ahead the official World Area Forecast System reaches. If your flight is further away, the page tells you the date and time your forecast opens. A new run arrives about every six hours, so a forecast for tonight is firmer than one for the day after tomorrow.

      The Jetcalm app's Pro tier adds an early outlook from 2 to 5 days ahead, estimated from the US Global Forecast System (GFS) weather model and labelled as less exact, because it changes more. On a long flight that takes off inside the 48 hours but lands after them, the last stretch can come from that estimate; it is marked "early outlook" on the timeline.

      The forecast maps stop at 75° north and south, so the far-northern stretch of a polar route (New York to Hong Kong, for example) shows as a gap: no data, not calm.

      A forecast, not a promise. Turbulence is uncomfortable, but it is not dangerous to the aircraft: airliners are built for far more than they meet in flight. Forecasts can be wrong and pilots change course and altitude on the day, using reports from the aircraft ahead. Keep your seatbelt fastened whenever you are seated and always follow the crew's instructions.
